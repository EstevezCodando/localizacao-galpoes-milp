\begin{abstract}
Machine learning is increasingly used to reduce the search space of combinatorial problems, by
ranking variables or choosing which part of a solution to re-optimize. We ask how much of the
benefit is due to learning and how much to the restriction itself, for the single-source
capacitated facility location problem, by pairing each learned component with a learning-free
counterpart of the same function under equal wall-clock budgets. We study an adaptive expansion
that solves growing restricted problems over ranked facilities and stops when reduced costs
certify optimality, and a cluster-oriented large neighborhood search that re-optimizes groups of
customers over residual capacities, for which every accepted move is feasible for the full
problem. Facilities are ranked by a graph neural network or by the linear relaxation; a variant
of kernel search is the classical baseline. In a pre-registered test on 139 instances unseen
during development, the expansion ranked by the linear relaxation had less than half the primal
integral of the solver on the full model on 64 synthetic instances (0.050 against 0.108,
$p<0.001$). Replacing that ranking by the learned one made the expansion significantly worse
(0.089, $p=0.004$ after Holm's correction), and no better than the full solver on instances
larger than those used for training. The neighborhood search did not change the primal integral
and lowered the final deviation by about half a percentage point, at the threshold of
significance. On 71 benchmark instances with known optima, the reduced-cost certificate proved
optimality before the full model was solved in 59. The results concern one trained ranker and one
machine, part of the test ran under contention, and the evidence on learned subproblem selection
is exploratory.
\end{abstract}
