\begin{abstract}
Machine learning is increasingly used to reduce the search space of combinatorial problems, by
ranking variables or choosing which part of a solution to re-optimize. We ask how much of the
benefit is due to learning and how much to the restriction itself, for the single-source
capacitated facility location problem, by pairing each learned component with a learning-free
counterpart of the same function under equal wall-clock budgets. We study an adaptive expansion
that solves growing restricted problems over ranked facilities and stops when reduced costs
certify optimality, and a cluster-oriented large neighborhood search that re-optimizes groups of
customers over residual capacities, for which every accepted move is feasible for the full
problem. Facilities are ranked by a graph neural network or by the linear relaxation; a variant
of kernel search is the classical baseline. A pre-registered test on 139 instances unseen
during development was run twice, on a workstation and on dedicated cloud cores, and a hypothesis
is called confirmed only if both rounds agree. On 64 synthetic instances the expansion ranked by
the linear relaxation had about half the primal integral of the solver on the full model in both
rounds (0.033 against 0.061 on dedicated hardware, $p<0.001$). Replacing that ranking by the
learned one made the primal integral significantly worse, by the same amount in both rounds
($+0.039$; $p=0.005$ after Holm's correction), and numerically above that of the full solver on dedicated
hardware. The neighborhood search did not change the primal integral, and its effect on the final
deviation was not replicated. On 71 benchmark instances with known optima, the reduced-cost
certificate proved optimality before the full model was solved in 66. The results concern one
trained ranker, and the evidence on learned subproblem selection is exploratory.
\end{abstract}
